## Module Overview

One of the main challenges of working with datasets is the complexity involved in analysing them and extracting the correct outcomes. Data can be categorised by volume, velocity, and variety, and as structured or unstructured.

Nowadays, companies frequently use data visualisation techniques to convert the knowledge derived from big data into business planning decisions, trends, and patterns. Through data visualisation, data is presented to users in a meaningful and interactive way, which helps them to:

- explore,
- learn,
- communicate,
- solve problems, and
- make decisions.

This module provides students with:

- a grounding in the theoretical concepts of interactive data visualisation and presentation,
- an understanding of the creative design process, and
- experience of tools and techniques for interactive visualisation.

### Module Learning Outcomes

On successful completion of this module, you will be able to:

1. **MO1:** Understand and apply the fundamental concepts and techniques in data visualisation.
2. **MO2:** Use specialist software, such as Tableau, to produce visual insights into data and present your findings to a non-specialist audience.

### 2.3 Micro-lecture: Key Points

As you watch the micro-lecture, reflect on the following:

- **Beauty:** the first demand of a beautiful visualisation is that it must be unique, fresh, and novel. It is very difficult, or even impossible, to achieve novelty with default graph formats. Standard forms and conventions have their benefits, as they are easy to create with Excel or Tableau, but they are also limiting.
- **Make it informative:** there are two main areas to consider, the intended message and the context of use.
- **Aesthetics:** aesthetics has been described as "the measurement of beauty". However, it is not only about beauty or vision, but about stirring any combination of the senses in a way that gives the viewer pleasure.
- **Make it effective:** analyse your data deeply before settling on a final question. A good practice is to pose small sets of problems in your mind while reading a dataset.
- **Highlight what matters most:** applying colour to different parts of your visualisation lets you tell a more effective story, one that engages your audience at an emotional level and captures their attention quickly.

### 3.3 Micro-lecture: Key Points

As you watch the micro-lecture, reflect on the following:

- **Word clouds:** a word cloud (also tag cloud, wordle, or weighted list) is a novel visual representation of text data. According to Oxford Dictionaries, a word cloud is an image composed of the words used in a particular text or subject, in which the size of each word indicates its frequency or importance.
- **Colour choice matters:** a good set of colours highlights the story you want the data to tell, while a poor one hides or distracts from the visualisation's purpose.
- **Three types of colour palette:**
  1. qualitative,
  2. sequential, and
  3. diverging.
- **Colour blindness:** about 4% of the population have some form of colour blindness. Avoid combining red and green in your charts, as people with strong colour vision deficiency (CVD) see both as brown.

### 6.3 Micro-lecture: Key Points

As you watch the micro-lecture, reflect on the following chart types:

- **Bullet chart:** a variation of a bar graph, useful for visually tracking performance against a target, with results displayed in a single column.
- **Waterfall chart:** illustrates how a starting value becomes a final value through a series of intermediate additions and subtractions.
- **Polar area chart:** shows values for three or more quantitative measures on the same display, using a radial (circular) layout of several equal-angled sectors.
- **Dot plot:** displays quantitative values for different primary category items, with a secondary categorical breakdown that enables, for example, regional comparisons.
- **Table chart:** displays quantitative values across the intersections of two categorical and/or discrete quantitative dimensions.
- **Ridgeline plot:** shows the distribution of a numeric value for several groups. Distributions can be represented with histograms or density plots, all aligned to the same horizontal scale and presented with a slight overlap.
- **Waffle chart:** shows how proportions of different constituent categories make up a whole, using a square display typically divided into 100 points arranged in a grid.
- **Stacked bar graph:** shows how a larger category is divided into smaller categories, and the relationship of each part to the total.
- **Marimekko chart:** a two-dimensional stacked bar chart that varies both height and width to display parts of a whole across two dimensions simultaneously.
- **Dendrogram:** a node-link diagram that displays hierarchical relationships across multiple tiers of categorical dimensions.
- **Sankey diagram:** represents the composition and flow of quantitative relationships between different major ordinal dimensions.

### 7.3 Micro-lecture: Key Points

- **Static visualisations** are commonly seen as infographics, and usually focus on a specific data story.
